    <p class="section__eyebrow">About</p>
    <h2 class="page-title">Patrick J. Kearns, PhD</h2>
    <p class="page-lede">
      I am a Research Assistant Professor in the Department of Biology at the University of
      Massachusetts Boston, where I study how microbial communities assemble, defend their hosts,
      and respond to environmental change. My work spans systems as different as amphibian skin,
      bat wings, and salt marsh sediment — united by the same core questions of who is there,
      what they're doing, and how that shapes the health of the organisms and ecosystems around them.
    </p>
    <p class="page-lede">
      I'm currently a research scientist in the <a href="https://www.woodhamslab.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Woodhams Lab</a>
      at UMass Boston, working as part of the <strong>RIBBiTR</strong> (Research on Infectious Bacterial
      and Fungal Threats to Amphibian Biodiversity) project, investigating how skin-associated bacteria
      and fungi protect amphibians from pathogens like the chytrid fungus <em>Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis</em>.
      Before joining the Woodhams Lab, I was an assistant professor and biology program director at
      Fisher College, and I completed a postdoc in the Shade Lab at Michigan State University studying
      microbial dormancy and succession.
    </p>
    <p class="page-lede">
      My doctoral work at Northeastern University, advised by Jennifer Bowen, examined how nutrient
      loading and diel cycles shape the composition and activity of salt marsh microbial communities —
      research that grew out of long-term fieldwork at the Plum Island Estuary.
    </p>
    <p class="page-lede">
      See the <a href="{{ '/research/' | relative_url }}">Research</a> page for current projects,
      <a href="{{ '/publications/' | relative_url }}">Publications</a> for the full record, and
      <a href="{{ '/contact/' | relative_url }}">Contact</a> to get in touch.
    </p>
